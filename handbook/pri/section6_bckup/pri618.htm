Important Factors and Parsimonious Prediction
Identify Important Factors The two problems discussed in the previous section (important factors and a parsimonious model) will be handled in parallel. Determination of one will yield the other. In regard to the "important factors", our immediate goal is to take the full subset of 7 main effects and interactions and extract a subset which we will declare as "important", with the complementary subset being "unimportant". Seven possible criteria are given in the discussion of the Yates technique.
Criterion for Including Terms in the Model Of the seven possible criterion, we applied the following five in this case study.
  1. Effects: Engineering Significance
  2. Effects: Order of Magnitude
  3. Effects: Statistical Significance
  4. Effects: Probability Plots
  5. Averages: Youden Plot
The last section summarizes the conclusions based on all of the applied criterion.
Effects: Engineering Significance For this criterion, we declare a factor as "important" if the effect is greater than some a priori declared engineering difference. In the absence of an a priori difference, a good rough rule for the minimum engineering significant is to keep only those factors whose effect is greater than, say, 10% of the current production average. In this case, the overall mean is 2.66, so a reasonable assumption is that the average detector has a sensitivity of about 2.5 ohms. This suggests that we would declare all factors whose effect is greater than 10% of 2.5 ohms = 0.25 ohm to be significant from an engineering point of view.

The minimum engineering significant difference criterion supports keeping two terms: X1 (3.10250) and X2 (-.86750).

Effects: Order of Magnitude For this criterion, we exclude any factor that is less than 10% of the maximun effect size. For the current case study, the largest effect is from factor 1 (3.10250 ohms), and so 10% of that is 0.31 ohms.

The order of magnitude criterion supports keeping two terms: X1 (3.10250) and X2 (-.86750). A third term, X2*X3 (.29750), is just under the cut-off.

Effects: Statistical Significance For the current case study:
  1. the engineer did not know ;
  2. the design (a 23 full factorial) did not have replication;
  3. ignoring 3-term interactions and higher leads to an estimate of based on omitting only a single term: the X1*X2*X3 interaction.
Thus for this current case study, if one assumes that the 3-term interaction is nil and hence represents a single drawing from a population centered at zero, then an estimate of the standard deviation of the effect is simply the effect itself (0.1425 from the Yates table). Two such effect standard deviations is 0.2850. This rule becomes to keep all > 0.2850. This results in keeping three terms: X1 (3.10250), X2 (-.86750), and X1*X2 (.29750).
Normal Probablity Plot of Effects and Halfnormal Probability Plot of Effects The following plot shows the normal probability plot of the effects and the halfnormal probability plot of the absolute value of the effects.

For the case study at hand, both probability plots clearly show two factors displaced off the line, and from the third plot (with factor tags included), we see that those two factors are factor 1 and factor 2. All of the remaining five effects are behaving like random drawings from a normal distribution centered at zero, and so are deemed to be statistically insignificant. In conclusion, this rule keeps two factors: X1 (3.10250) and X2 (-.86750).

For the case study at hand, the Youden plot clearly shows a cluster of points near the grand average (2.65875) with two displaced points above (factor 1) and below (factor 2). Based on the Youden plot, we thus conclude to keep two factors: X1 (3.10250) and X2 (-.86750).
