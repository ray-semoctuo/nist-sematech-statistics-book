5.6.1. Eddy Current Probe Sensitivity Case Study

5.6.1.11.

Final Conclusions
Goals of This Case Study The output and goals of this case study were:
  1. Determine the most important factors.
  2. Determine the best settings for the factors.
  3. Determine a good predicition equation for the data.
Most Important Factors and Prediction Equation The various dex plots and Yates analysis showed that the number of turns (X1) and the winding distance (X2) were the most important factors. The Yates analysis gave us a good prediction equation for the data

Best Settings At this point it is good to review the conclusions regarding goal 2 (determine best settings) for this case study. If we are trying to determine settings of the factors which maximize the response, as in the this eddy current case study, then there are several approaches which lay claim title to yielding "best" settings:

  1. Average-based
  2. Model-based
  3. Data-based

Methods 1 and 3 yield answers within the current -1 and +1 domain of the data. Method 2 yields an answer that frequently extrapolates beyond the domain of the executed experiment

This eddy current case study was drawn from an research and development environment, and so heavier emphasis was placed on understanding and insight (that is, what factors are important) than with best settings. On the other hand, in a production environment, both goals are still in play, but the emphasis goes slightly heavier to the best settings goal as opposed to the important factors goal. In a general engineering environment (either research and development or production), the above two goals frequently have, in practice, much greater interest to the engineer than the third enumerated goal (good model/prediction equation).

What then, are the final recommended best settings for this case study, and how do they compare with one another?

Average Based From the main effects plot (or equivalently from the signs of the estimated effects), it was seen that the comparison of the average response for each individual factor at the -1 and + levels, and the choosing of the setting that yielded the larger average response yielded

    (X1 = +1, X2 = -1, X3 = +1)

From step 1 of the contour plot analysis, we saw that the best of the four vertices for the two dominant factors (X1 and X2):

    (X1 = +1, X2 = -1)

which agrees in part with the above seetings but leaves the question of X3 undone.

Model Based Also from the contour plot, we were able to draw 2-factor model-based contour curves which yielded

    (X1 = +2, X2 = -2, X3 = ?)

as the global estimate of the best setting (with the setting of X3 left undetermined).

Data Based Finally, a different, assumption-free, and simple (= no estimation and no modeling) data-based estimate of the best settings is always of interest to pursue. If one takes the decidedly anti-statistical attitude that all of the previously-described statistical estimation and statistical modeling is nice but is a bit artificial and indirect, and that the only thing real is the eight data values on hand, then an argument could be forwarded that a simple sort of the eight data values (carrying along the three factor settings) should yield interesting information and optimal values all by itself. Proceeding in this fashion, such a simple sort (smallest to largest) of the data was carried out, yielding: 

    Y         X1          X2          X3
  Probe     Number      Winding      Wire
Impedance  of Turns     Distance     Guage
-------------------------------------------
   0.55       -1          +1          -1
   0.67       -1          +1          +1
   1.51       -1          -1          +1
   1.70       -1          -1          -1
   3.39       +1          +1          -1
   4.29       +1          +1          +1

   4.59       +1          -1          +1
The following conclusions can be drawn from the sort:

  1. Best Settings: The largest response is 4.59 and so the corresponding factor settings:

      (X1 = +1, X2 = -1, X3 = +1)

    can lay valid claim to being the best settings. Note interestingly enough that worst data point (0.55) has the exact opposite signs:

      (X1 = -1, X2 = +1, X3 = -1)

    which further reinforces our choice for the (+1,-1,+1) best setting. That happened in this case; it does not happen in most cases.

  2. Important factors: Note that the largest value (4.59) has settings (+,-,+). Note that the next largest (and nearly identical) value is (4.57) and it has settings (+,-,-). The 2 common settings for the 2 largest values are X1 (+) and X2 (-) which supports the fact that they are indeed important factors, while X3 is relegated to being an unimportant factor-- as per agreement with the extensive previous statistical analyses.

    Note also that in the sorted table, the four largest values all had X1 = +1 and the four smallest values had X1 = -1, thus reaffirming that factor X1 is in fact the most important factor. Further, within the largest four responses with X1 = +1, note that X2 is +1 for the two largest values and -1 for the next two values. Similarly, for the four smallest values, the same pattern for X2 (two +'s followed by two -'s) persist, thus supporting the prior conclusion that X2 is the second most important factor.

Comparing Best Settings: Average Based Versus Data Based Based on the averages, the derived best setting was (+ -,+). Based on the sorted data, the derived best setting was (+,-,+). Fortuitously in this case, the two different approaches yielded the same conclusion, and so our final unambiguous estimate for the best setting within the domain of the data is

    (X1 = +1, X2 = -1, X3 = +1)

Because two different methods converged to the same conclusion, our confidence in the validity of that conclusion is greatly enhanced.

In other case studies, the two approaches may not agree in all of the settings. In this author's experience, however, the two methods will always agree for the important factors (factors X1 and X2 here) and disagreement (if any) shows up in the unimportant factors. In such cases, the recommendation course of action is to

  1. Important factors: Use the common best settings as given by either and both approaches.

  2. Unimportant Factors:

    1. If there is a difference in cost or set-up time for the unimportant factors, then use the least expensive or most hassle-free settings for these factors.

    2. If there is no difference in factor cost or set-up time, and the analyst must decide on the average-based settings for the unimportant factors, or the data-based settings for the unimportant factors, then this author would recommend using the average-based for the following two reasons:

      1. The scope is broader for average-based. The settings depend on averages across two or more observations, as opposed to having the noisiness of just a single observation in the data-based method.

      2. The settings for the unimportant factors will always exist for full factorial designs (as is our present case study) but for other studies using a fractional factorial design, the use of average-based estimates for the settings of unimportant factors can yield settings for which data may not yet exist, and so is a step beyond the settings based just on the sorted data.
