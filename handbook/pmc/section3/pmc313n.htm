6.3.1.3. Cusum Control Charts 
CUSUM is an efficient alternative to Shewhart procedures CUSUM charts, while not as intuitive and simple to operate as Shewhart charts, have been shown to be more efficient in detecting small shifts in the mean of a process. 

CUSUM works as follows: Let us collect k samples, each of size n,and  compute the mean of each sample. Then the cumulative sum (CUSUM) control chart is formed by plotting one of the following quantities: 
Definition of cumulative Sum and the V-mask
against the sample number m, where m0 is the target for the process mean and  is the known (or estimated) standard deviation of the sample means. The choice of which of these two quantities is plotted is usually determioned by the statistical software package. In either case, as long as the process remains in control centered around m0, the cusum plot will show variation in a random pattern centered about zero. If the process mean shifts upward, the charted cusum points will eventually drift upwards, and vice-versa if the process mean decreases.     

In order to determine whether a process is out of control we use the V Mask, proposed by Barnhard in 1959. A V Mask is a graph in the form of a V on its side that is superimposed on the graph of the cumulative sums. 


The distance d, between the vertical line and the apex of the V is called the lead-distance. The angle between either arm and d is referred to as theta (q). q and d are the parameters of the V Mask. 
It works as follows: Place  the V Mask with its vertical axis on the  last point. If all of the previous points lie within the two arms of the mask, the process is in control. Otherwise (even if one point lies outside) the process is out of control. 

The performance of the Cusum Chart is determined by the parameters of the V mask. The V Mask is defined by the lead distance, d, and the angle between the arms, q.

Since not all the data points lie within the arms of the V Mask, the process is out of control as of the time of the last point. 

Analyzing ARL's for CUSUM control  charts shows that they are better than Shewhart control charts when it is desired to detect small shifts (2 sigma or less) of the mean.
